Editing Drone Photos: How to Recover Dynamic Range

Recovering dynamic range in drone photography is essential for balancing harsh, bright skies with dark landscapes below. Because drone sensors are relatively small compared to full-frame ground cameras, they often struggle with extreme light contrast. This guide covers practical, step-by-step techniques to restore lost highlight and shadow details using standard editing software, ensuring your aerial shots look natural, sharp, and properly exposed.

1. Always Shoot and Edit in RAW

Dynamic range recovery begins before the editing phase. Standard JPEG files discard up to 80% of color and exposure data through compression. RAW files (such as DNG) retain the uncompressed sensor data, giving you the necessary latitude to pull details out of deep shadows and bring back information from clipped highlights without immediately introducing digital banding or artifacting.

2. Balance Global Highlights and Shadows

Open your RAW file in your editing software (such as Lightroom, Photoshop, or Capture One) and start with global tone corrections:

  • Lower the Highlights: Pull the Highlights slider down (often between -40 and -80) to recover cloud textures and sky detail.
  • Lift the Shadows: Push the Shadows slider up (typically between +30 and +60) to reveal ground features, trees, and structures.
  • Set the White and Black Points: Hold the Alt/Option key while dragging the Whites slider up until clipping just starts to appear, then drag the Blacks slider down until the darkest areas anchor into true black. This restores contrast so the image does not appear flat or washed out.

3. Use Targeted Local Masks

Global adjustments affect the entire frame, which can make the sky look unnaturally dark or make foreground foliage look muddy. Use local masking tools for precise control:

  • Linear Gradient on the Sky: Apply a linear gradient starting from the top down to the horizon. Slightly lower the exposure, increase contrast, and add a small amount of Dehaze to restore atmospheric clarity.
  • Radial or Brush Mask on the Foreground: Select the landscape to subtly increase warmth, exposure, and sharpness without blowing out the sky.

4. Mitigate Shadow Noise

Aggressively pulling details out of dark areas reveals sensor noise, especially on drones equipped with 1/2.3-inch or 1-inch sensors.

  • Zoom in to 100% on the lifted shadow areas.
  • Apply targeted Luminance Noise Reduction just enough to smooth out grain without softening fine textures.
  • Use AI-based denoise tools if available, as they effectively differentiate between drone sensor noise and actual terrain details.

5. Blend Bracketed Exposures for Extreme Contrast

When a single RAW file does not contain enough dynamic range to recover both the sun and deep ground shadows, use Auto Exposure Bracketing (AEB) files shot during flight:

  • Select the bracketed set (typically 3 or 5 exposures taken at different shutter speeds).
  • Merge them using an HDR (High Dynamic Range) merge function.
  • This generates a 32-bit composite file containing the complete tonal range of the scene, allowing you to edit the exposure seamlessly without noise or highlight blowout.
