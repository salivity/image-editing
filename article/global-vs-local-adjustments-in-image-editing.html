Global vs Local Adjustments in Image Editing

Mastering the balance between global and local adjustments is essential for creating clean, professional, and visually engaging photographs. Global adjustments alter the entire frame simultaneously to establish a cohesive baseline, while local adjustments target specific zones to refine details and direct the viewer's focus. Knowing precisely when to deploy each technique streamlines your post-processing workflow, prevents over-editing, and ensures your final image looks natural and balanced.

Understanding Global Adjustments

Global adjustments apply changes uniformly across the entire photograph. Every pixel in the frame is affected by the same set of parameters, regardless of where it is located.

Common global edits include:

  • Setting the overall white balance and tint
  • Establishing base exposure and contrast
  • Applying lens profile corrections to remove distortion and vignetting
  • Setting initial sharpening and noise reduction profiles

When to Apply Global Adjustments

Global adjustments should almost always be executed at the very beginning of your editing process. They serve as the foundation upon which the rest of your edits rely.

  • Correcting Capture Errors: Fix technical inconsistencies from the shoot, such as an improper color temperature, under- or over-exposure across the scene, or lens optical flaws.
  • Creating a Unified Mood: If a scene needs to feel warmer, cooler, or more dramatic overall, apply these baseline color and tonal shifts globally so the atmosphere remains consistent throughout the entire frame.
  • Batch Processing: When editing a series of photos taken in the same lighting conditions, global adjustments can be copied and pasted across the entire set to save time.

Understanding Local Adjustments

Local adjustments modify only designated regions of an image, leaving the surrounding areas untouched. Modern editing software uses tools like radial filters, linear gradients, brush tools, and AI-driven subject masks to isolate these areas.

Common local edits include:

  • Dodging (brightening) and burning (darkening) specific elements
  • Enhancing the eyes or smoothing skin in portraits
  • Lowering exposure in an overly bright sky without darkening the landscape
  • Boosting saturation on a single focal element to make it stand out

When to Apply Local Adjustments

Local adjustments should be introduced after the global baseline is fully established. They are precision tools meant for storytelling, problem-solving, and directing attention.

  • Guiding Viewer Attention: The human eye naturally gravitates toward the brightest, sharpest, and most contrast-heavy parts of an image. Use local exposure and clarity adjustments to illuminate your primary subject while subtly dimming distracting background elements.
  • Balancing High-Contrast Scenes: In situations where the dynamic range exceeds the camera sensor's capabilities—such as a backlit portrait or a bright sky over a shadowed mountain—use graduated or brush masks to lift shadows or recover highlights only where needed.
  • Targeted Retouching: Remove blemishes, reduce glare on glasses, or whiten teeth without altering the skin tones and colors of the surrounding environment.

The Standard Workflow: Global First, Local Second

Always adjust globally before refining locally. Modifying specific sections of an image before correcting the overall exposure and color temperature often leads to redundant work. For example, if you spend time brightening a subject's face using a local mask, only to later realize the entire photograph is underexposed, raising the global exposure will make your earlier local adjustment appear overly bright and unnatural.

By perfecting the overall balance of the image first, you minimize the amount of local intervention required to produce a polished final product.
