How to Replace Closed Eyes in Photos with Image Editing

Blinking at the moment a camera shutter clicks is a common issue in portrait and group photography, but it can be easily corrected by borrowing an open eye from an alternate shot. This guide outlines the standard workflow for replacing a closed eye using raster image editing software like Adobe Photoshop, GIMP, or Affinity Photo. By following the core steps of selecting, aligning, masking, and color-matching, you can create a seamless, undetectable fix that rescues an otherwise ruined photograph.

1. Select the Best Source Image

Find an alternate photo of the same person taken around the same time. The closer the match in head angle, facial expression, and lighting direction, the more realistic the final result will be. Avoid using photos taken in radically different lighting conditions or from distinct camera angles, as mismatched shadows and facial perspective will make the composite look artificial.

2. Copy the Open Eye

Open both the target image (the one with the closed eyes) and the source image in your editor. Using a loose selection tool—such as the Lasso tool—draw around the open eye, including the eyelid, the eyebrow, and a small buffer of surrounding skin. Copy this selection and paste it as a new layer directly above your target image.

3. Align and Transform

Position the new eye layer over the closed eye. To align it accurately:

  • Temporarily reduce the opacity of the new eye layer to approximately 50%.
  • Use the Move and Free Transform tools (scale, rotate, or warp) to align the inner and outer corners (canthi) of the eye, as well as the tear duct and brow line, with the underlying facial structure.
  • Check the scale carefully to ensure the new eye is proportional to the rest of the face.
  • Once positioned, return the layer opacity to 100%.

4. Blend Using a Layer Mask

Hard edges will immediately reveal the edit. To blend the patch seamlessly:

  • Add a Layer Mask to the eye patch layer.
  • Select a soft-edged brush set to black with low-to-medium flow or opacity.
  • Paint on the mask to conceal the hard outer boundaries of the pasted layer, leaving only the eye, lid crease, and essential surrounding skin visible.
  • If you erase too much, switch your brush color to white to paint the details back in.

5. Match Lighting and Tone

Even from the same photo session, slight differences in exposure or skin tone can occur. Fine-tune the patch layer to integrate it with the base image:

  • Curves or Levels: Clip an adjustment layer to the eye patch layer to match the contrast and brightness of the subject's face.
  • Color Balance or Hue/Saturation: Adjust the color temperature if the skin around the replaced eye appears too red, yellow, or pale compared to the rest of the face.
  • Noise Matching: If the source photo has a different ISO or grain texture, apply a minimal amount of artificial grain or blur to the patch layer so the resolution matches the target image.

6. Correct Catchlights and Final Polish

Zoom in closely to inspect the catchlight—the white reflection of the light source inside the pupil and iris. Ensure the catchlights in both eyes match in direction, shape, and intensity. If the reflection in the grafted eye points the wrong way relative to the other eye, use a small clone stamp or brush tool to reposition it. Finally, zoom out to 100% and view the face as a whole to confirm that the symmetry and expression appear natural.
