Brush Flow vs Opacity: What Is the Difference?

In digital image editing software like Adobe Photoshop, both opacity and flow control the transparency and intensity of brush strokes, but they function fundamentally differently. Opacity sets a strict maximum limit on the transparency of an entire stroke, requiring you to release the brush before you can build up more pigment. Flow, on the other hand, determines the rate at which paint is applied to the canvas, allowing you to layer and intensify the color continuously within a single continuous stroke.

Understanding Brush Opacity

Opacity dictates the overall transparency ceiling of a brush stroke. If you set the brush opacity to 50%, the paint will never exceed 50% opacity in a single stroke, no matter how many times you pass the cursor back and forth over the same spot without releasing the mouse button or lifting your stylus.

To build up greater density using opacity, you must:

  1. Make a stroke at your chosen opacity level.
  2. Release the mouse button or lift the pen from the tablet.
  3. Draw a second stroke over the top of the first.

Because of this behavior, opacity creates flat, consistent layers of tone that only darken where separate, distinct strokes overlap.

Understanding Brush Flow

Flow acts like the valve on an airbrush or a spray paint can. Rather than placing a cap on maximum transparency, it controls how quickly pigment is laid down as you move the cursor.

If you set the flow to 10%, a single pass lays down a faint 10% density. However, if you keep the mouse button pressed and sweep the brush back and forth over the exact same area, the paint continuously accumulates until it reaches 100% density. You do not need to lift the stylus or start a new stroke to build up color.

Key Differences at a Glance

  • Pigment Buildup: Opacity will not build up upon itself within a single continuous stroke. Flow continuously builds up density as long as the brush moves over the same area.
  • Control Style: Opacity relies on separate, stacked strokes to build tone. Flow relies on brush speed, pressure, and repeated passes within a single stroke.
  • Edge Blending: Opacity often leaves visible hard boundaries between overlapping strokes. Flow creates soft, seamless transitions and gradual gradients because tone accumulates organically.

When to Use Opacity vs. Flow

Use Opacity when you need uniform coverage, crisp layering, or precise control over transparency. It is ideal for masking, blocking in flat colors, creating semi-transparent overlays, and working with digital inks where consistency is critical.

Use Flow for tasks that require gradual transitions and blending. It is the preferred setting for digital painting, photorealistic shading, portrait retouching (such as dodging and burning), and smoothing skin tones, as it mimics the natural behavior of traditional painting mediums.
