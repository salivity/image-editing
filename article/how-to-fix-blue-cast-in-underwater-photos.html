How to Fix Blue Cast in Underwater Photos

Deep-water photography frequently suffers from an overwhelming blue or cyan cast because water absorbs warm wavelengths of light—starting with red—within just a few meters of the surface. Correcting this imbalance in post-processing requires a systematic approach to reconstruct lost color information and balance tonal values. By shooting in RAW and leveraging white balance controls, individual RGB curves, HSL panels, and targeted masks, you can neutralize the heavy tint and restore the natural colors of marine subjects.

1. Shoot in RAW and Maximize White Balance

The most effective way to address severe color casts is to capture images in RAW format. RAW files retain uncompressed sensor data, providing the latitude needed to push color values without extreme artifacting.

  • Temperature: Push the Temperature slider toward the warm (amber) end of the spectrum. In deep shots, you may need to push it significantly higher than normal settings.
  • Tint: Drag the Tint slider toward magenta. Because the dominant cast is cyan/blue, adding magenta directly counteracts the excess green-blue spectrum.

2. Manually Rebuild the Red Channel with Tone Curves

When white balance sliders hit their limits, use the Curves tool for precise channel-by-channel adjustments.

  • Red Curve: Click on the Red channel. Place an anchor point in the middle (midtones) and push it upward to inject red back into the scene. Lift the shadow anchor slightly to prevent the deepest areas from remaining murky cyan.
  • Blue and Green Curves: Switch to the Blue channel and slightly lower the midtones or highlights to remove excess blue glare. Adjust the Green channel downwards if the water leans overly turquoise.

3. Fine-Tune with the HSL/Color Panel

After the global cast is balanced, secondary colors often look distorted. Use the Hue, Saturation, and Luminance (HSL) panel to refine specific zones:

  • Saturation: Lower the saturation of Blues and Cyans to prevent them from overpowering the subject. If marine life looks pale, selectively boost the saturation of Oranges and Yellows.
  • Luminance: Darken the Blue and Cyan luminance slightly to add depth back into open-water backgrounds, which often look blown out after global warming adjustments.
  • Hue: Shift Cyans toward pure Blue to create a more natural ocean background, rather than an unnatural neon-greenish cast.

4. Restore Depth and Contrast

Color correction often washes out contrast or introduces digital haze in the shadows.

  • Dehaze and Clarity: Apply moderate amounts of the Dehaze tool to cut through the light-scattering effect of suspended particles in the water.
  • Black Point: Drop the Black slider or deepen the lower end of the master RGB curve to ensure your shadows have a true, deep anchor.

5. Apply Localized Adjustments

Water depth and distance from the lens dictate how much red light was lost. Foreground elements lit by ambient light or strobes require different settings than the distant open water behind them. Use radial or linear gradient masks to warm up close-up subjects while keeping the background water deep blue, preventing the entire image from taking on an unnatural, muddy appearance.
