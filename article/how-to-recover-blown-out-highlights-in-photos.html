How to Recover Blown-Out Highlights in Photos

Blown-out highlights occur when a camera sensor clips bright areas to pure white, resulting in a loss of texture, color, and detail. While fully clipped digital data cannot always be perfectly restored, several advanced editing workflows can salvage partially clipped data or realistically reconstruct missing details. This guide covers the most effective techniques for recovering blown-out highlights, ranging from basic RAW exposure adjustments and tone curves to advanced luminosity masking, channel blending, texture synthesis, and AI-driven generative fill.

1. RAW Highlight and White Clipping Pullback

If you shot in RAW format, the file likely contains latent dynamic range that is hidden by the default rendering.

  • Highlight Slider: In raw processors like Adobe Camera Raw, Lightroom, or Capture One, lower the Highlights slider first. This acts globally on the upper tonal regions without drastically affecting midtones.
  • Whites Slider: Hold the Alt (Windows) or Option (Mac) key while dragging the Whites slider to the left until the clipping warnings disappear. This resets the absolute white point and prevents harsh transitions.
  • Exposure: Lower overall exposure slightly, then use shadow and midtone sliders to compensate for lost brightness in the rest of the frame.

2. Targeted Luminosity Masking

When global adjustments make the rest of the image look flat or muddy, use local adjustments tied to a luminosity mask.

  • Create a mask targeted strictly at the 85% to 100% luminance range.
  • Apply a localized drop in exposure, highlights, and contrast.
  • Add a slight warm tint or match the surrounding color temperature within the mask to remove the unnatural grayish cast that often appears when desaturated, overexposed highlights are lowered.

3. Curve Flattening and De-Hazing

Overexposed areas frequently suffer from low contrast and optical glare from the lens.

  • Use a Tone Curve (Parametric or Point Curve) and pull the top-right point down slightly, or introduce a gentle downward curve in the highlight quadrant to compress the tonal range.
  • Use a local brush or radial gradient over the blown area with a low value of Dehaze or Clarity. This restores micro-contrast along the edges of the clipped zone.

4. Color Channel Reconstruction

Often, only one or two color channels (typically red or green) are fully clipped, while the remaining channel still contains structural data.

  • In Photoshop, inspect the individual Red, Green, and Blue channels via the Channels panel. Identify the channel that retains the most texture.
  • Duplicate that channel, adjust its contrast, and use it as a selection mask or paste it onto a new layer set to Luminosity blend mode.
  • Sample the native color from adjacent, properly exposed areas using an eyedropper, set a blank layer to Color blend mode, and brush over the clipped zone to restore natural hue without disturbing the newly added tonal values.

5. Texture Transfer with Frequency Separation

If the highlight is clipped to pure 255,255,255 white, no slider can recover detail because no digital data exists. You must borrow texture from neighboring areas.

  • Separate the image into high-frequency (texture) and low-frequency (color and tone) layers.
  • On the low-frequency layer, sample the ambient tone from surrounding pixels and paint a smooth gradient over the blown area to establish base color.
  • On the high-frequency layer, use the Clone Stamp tool (set to Current Layer) to clone real texture—such as skin pores, cloud edges, or fabric weave—from an adjacent, well-exposed zone directly into the clipped area.

6. AI Inpainting and Generative Fill

For completely lost areas where manual texture synthesis is too complex—such as large sections of sky or intricate architectural elements—generative AI provides the most seamless recovery.

  • Make a loose selection around the clipped highlights with a feathered edge (2 to 5 pixels).
  • Apply generative inpainting without entering any prompt, or use a descriptive prompt like "natural cloud texture" or "subtle sky gradient."
  • Generative engines analyze the contextual boundaries and synthesize mathematically consistent details, replacing empty white pixels with believable lighting and detail.
